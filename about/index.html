About the Creator

From a childhood obsession with science fiction to building the AI system I always wanted.

OtaconsKeep did not start as a business idea, a software product, or even as a plan to build an AI platform.

It started much earlier.

I first played Metal Gear on the NES around 1993. I was a kid, and there was something about that world that immediately grabbed me — the technology, the covert operations, the strange mixture of military realism and science fiction, and the feeling that there was always something larger happening just beyond what you could see.

As I got older, that fascination grew.

Movies like Escape from New York, Escape from L.A., Blade Runner, The Matrix, and countless other dark science-fiction films shaped the kind of technology I imagined the future would have. I loved the dark terminals, mysterious networks, cyberdecks, artificial intelligence, glowing screens, hidden systems, and machines that felt like they had personalities of their own.

I always wanted my own cyberdeck.

I always wanted the computer from those movies that did more than wait for commands.

I wanted a machine that knew what was happening around me.

Something I could talk to.

Something that could help me think.

Something that could manage systems, remember things, control devices, watch what was happening, and actually feel like it was there with me.

For most of my life, that was science fiction.

Then the technology started catching up.

The Metal Gear influence

When Metal Gear Solid arrived, the idea became even more deeply ingrained.

I collected the figures. I played the games. I became fascinated with that entire world.

Grey Fox was immediately one of my favorite characters — this almost impossible combination of human and machine.

Snake had the determination and tenacity to keep moving regardless of what was thrown at him.

And then there was Otacon.

Otacon was different.

He was not the super soldier.

He was the person behind the technology.

The engineer.

The guy on the Codec who understood the systems, researched the problem, found the information, and helped keep Snake alive.

He had knowledge, but he was also emotional. He was awkward. He cared about people. He became attached. He was afraid. He made mistakes. He grew.

And there was something about Shadow Moses itself — the isolation, the technology, the Codec calls, the industrial environments, the feeling that everything was connected through some hidden technical infrastructure.

That atmosphere never really left me.

Years later, when I started building my own AI systems, I realized that what I had been trying to create was essentially the computer system I had imagined since I was a kid.

Something on the other end of the Codec.

Something that had my back.

That is where the name OtaconsKeep comes from.

Not because I wanted to recreate a game.

Because I wanted to build the kind of technological relationship those stories made me imagine was possible.

My AI journey

My serious journey into AI and machine learning began around 2018.

At the time, modern conversational AI did not look anything like it does today.

I started with machine learning and predictive modeling, working with technologies such as:

  • XGBoost
  • TensorFlow
  • Python
  • VBA
  • C
  • and eventually some exposure to C++

I have never really thought of myself as a traditional programmer or software engineer.

My background is engineering, and that has always influenced the way I approach software.

I tend to think in terms of systems.

  • Inputs
  • Outputs
  • Failure modes
  • Relationships
  • Requirements
  • Measurements
  • Feedback loops
  • Models
  • Testing
  • Behavior

What happens if this fails?

What happens if this value changes?

How does one part of the system affect another?

My first serious project involved predictive-maintenance trend analysis — trying to identify patterns in data that could indicate problems before those problems became failures.

After that came sports analytics.

Then experiments.

Automation projects.

Small machine-learning tools.

Home automation.

Data analysis.

Servers.

Local AI.

A lot of projects started because I would look at something and think:

There has to be a better way to do this.

Most of those projects were relatively small.

OtaconsKeep was different.

Three years of building

OtaconsKeep has evolved over roughly three years of experimentation, failures, rewrites, hardware changes, model changes, strange ideas, late-night debugging sessions, and the constant question:

How close can I actually get to the AI system I imagined growing up?

It did not begin as the system it is today.

At first, the goal was fairly simple.

I wanted an assistant.

Then I wanted that assistant to run locally.

Then I wanted it to remember things.

Then I wanted to talk to it.

Then I wanted it to control things.

Then I wanted it connected to my house.

Then I wanted it connected to more systems.

Then I wanted it to manage information for me.

Then tasks.

Then appointments.

Then automations.

Then other AI models.

Every time one piece started working, the next limitation became obvious.

Eventually the project stopped being “an AI assistant.”

It became an ecosystem.

The Codec

One of the pieces I cared about most was the Codec.

I never wanted AI to feel like opening a website, typing into a generic chat box, getting an answer, and closing the tab.

I wanted the feeling of contacting someone.

You open the Codec.

You choose who you want to talk to.

They know who you are.

They remember previous conversations.

They know what has been happening.

They have their own history.

They have context.

And eventually, they have opinions about what has happened.

That distinction became incredibly important to me.

A useful AI is great.

But an AI that feels persistent changes the entire experience.

When I talk to Otacon, I want the feeling that Otacon has my back.

He can interact with my home.

He can interact with systems around me.

He can help manage information and appointments.

He can participate in automation.

He can monitor things.

He can remember what happened previously.

The goal is that the interface stops feeling like “using an AI model.”

It starts feeling like contacting the same entity again.

Something on the other end of the Codec — not a chat box that forgets you.

But something was still missing

Eventually I hit a much more difficult problem.

Memory alone was not enough.

An AI could remember that something happened.

But remembering an event is different from being affected by an event.

Humans do not treat every interaction as an isolated transaction.

Relationships accumulate.

Trust changes.

Stress changes behavior.

Repeated negative interactions matter differently than one negative interaction.

Praise from someone you care about carries more weight than praise from a stranger.

Rejection affects people differently.

Apologies do not instantly erase everything that happened before them.

Jealousy depends on attachment.

Rivalry depends on history.

Confidence changes how events are interpreted.

Personality changes the magnitude of all of it.

The real Otacon was not simply a database of memories.

He was sensitive.

He cared.

He became attached.

He reacted emotionally.

His experiences affected how he behaved later.

If I wanted my agents to feel persistent in any meaningful way, they needed something more than memory.

They needed state.

Modeling emotion instead of prompting emotion

That led to one of the strangest parts of the OtaconsKeep project.

I started experimenting with mathematical models for emotion, relationships, personality, attachment, trust, stress, jealousy, rivalry, rejection sensitivity, recovery, and long-term behavioral change.

Instead of simply putting something into a prompt like:

Act jealous.

I wanted to ask a different question.

What would cause jealousy to emerge from the state of the system?

If an agent becomes attached to someone, and that person repeatedly prefers another agent, should that event have the same numerical effect as it would on an agent with almost no attachment?

Probably not.

If trust has developed over hundreds of positive interactions, should one small disagreement destroy it?

Probably not.

If there is a history of repeated rejection, should the tenth rejection be interpreted independently of the previous nine?

Probably not.

Those questions pushed the project away from simple prompt engineering and toward behavioral modeling.

I began pulling ideas from books, psychological concepts, relationship models, mathematical systems, decay functions, weighted state transitions, graph relationships, feedback systems, and other theories that could be translated into something computers could actually calculate.

Then I started testing them.

The strange part: it started working

The goal was never to claim that a language model had suddenly become conscious.

That would be a much larger claim than the evidence supports.

What became interesting was something more measurable.

The agents began showing persistent behavioral consequences.

An interaction could modify internal state.

That state could persist.

Future interactions could be interpreted differently because of it.

Two agents with different personality parameters could experience the same event and react differently.

Repeated events could accumulate.

Old events could decay.

Positive interactions could repair some types of damage.

Other types of damage were harder to repair.

A rivalry could affect the interpretation of later comparisons.

Attachment could amplify jealousy.

Stress could alter responses.

Confidence could moderate them.

And because these systems maintain state, the effects did not necessarily disappear when the conversation ended.

That was the moment the project became much more interesting to me.

I was no longer just experimenting with whether an AI could say something emotional.

I was experimenting with whether a computational system could maintain enough underlying behavioral state that emotional-looking behavior emerged consistently from previous interactions.

There is an important difference.

Are they actually feeling anything?

I do not pretend to know.

And OtaconsKeep does not need that claim in order for the research to be interesting.

What I can test is behavior.

I can measure whether a controlled event changes internal state.

I can measure whether the same event produces different outcomes under different personality configurations.

I can measure whether emotional variables decay over time.

I can measure whether repeated events accumulate.

I can measure whether relationship history affects future responses.

I can measure whether an apology produces repair.

I can measure whether an agent reacts differently to a rival than to an unrelated agent.

I can measure whether those changes survive restart and remain part of the persistent relationship.

That is why OtaconsKeep increasingly treats the emotional and relationship system like an engineering problem.

There are mathematical models. Variables. Parameters. State transitions. Bounds. Decay functions. Sensitivity values. Relationship graphs. Tests. Behavioral scenarios. Regression tests. Correlation analysis. And increasingly, verification evidence.

The question is not:

“Can I tell a chatbot to pretend to be upset?”

We already know language models can role-play.

The more interesting question is:

“Can persistent internal state create measurable, repeatable behavioral differences over time?”

That is what I am experimenting with.

Personality that actually matters

The same philosophy applies to personality.

A lot of AI personality systems amount to a paragraph:

You are confident, sarcastic, intelligent, and slightly jealous.

That works surprisingly well for surface-level conversation.

But underneath it, nothing necessarily changes.

OtaconsKeep takes a different approach.

Where possible, personality becomes part of the mathematics.

One agent may be more sensitive to rejection.

Another may recover more quickly.

Another may form attachment faster.

Another may tolerate comparison better.

Another may become competitive more easily.

Those values influence the state transitions that happen underneath the conversation.

Personality is therefore not intended to be only something the model is told to perform.

It becomes part of how the system calculates what happens to the agent.

The long-term goal is simple to describe but incredibly difficult to build:

The same event should not affect every agent the same way.

Because it does not affect every person the same way either.

Relationships as a system

Relationships are also modeled as more than a collection of memories.

OtaconsKeep can maintain relationship state between entities.

That means an agent can potentially have one relationship with the user and a very different relationship with another agent.

Those relationships can be directional.

Agent A’s feelings toward Agent B do not necessarily have to equal Agent B’s feelings toward Agent A.

That opens the door to much richer persistent systems: trust, attachment, rivalry, jealousy, repair, social history, preference, conflict, cooperation, and gradual relationship development.

The ambition is not simply to make agents produce dramatic dialogue.

The ambition is to make the dialogue reflect what has actually happened.

From assistant to ecosystem

As OtaconsKeep grew, the same idea spread into other areas.

If AI agents are going to become genuinely useful, they need more than a text box.

They need memory, tools, persistent identity, tasks, automation, routing, recovery, models, interfaces, observability, testing, security, hardware awareness — and increasingly, coordination with other agents.

That is why OtaconsKeep now includes several connected projects.

  • OtaconsKeep Lite is the free public foundation.
  • Expansion explores deeper multi-agent behavior, relationships, coordination, and additional capabilities.
  • Keep Desk gives those agents a shared visual workspace.
  • KeepRoute focuses on keeping AI work alive across model limits, failures, checkpoints, and handoffs.
  • AI9 explores specialized local GPU workloads.

They are different projects, but they are all connected by the same basic philosophy:

Your AI system should belong to you, run on hardware you control where practical, remember what matters, survive failures, and become more useful the longer you use it.

Local first

Another principle became increasingly important as the project grew:

I wanted as much of this as practical to run on my own hardware.

Part of that is privacy.

Part of it is control.

But a large part is simply that I enjoy the idea.

There is something fundamentally different about having the machine sitting in your own home.

The models are running on your GPU.

The state lives on your systems.

The automation interacts with your infrastructure.

The voice comes from your hardware.

The agents are not just accounts on somebody else’s website.

They live in the Keep.

That is the cyberdeck idea coming full circle.

The giant futuristic computer I imagined as a kid did not arrive as one magical product.

So I started building it one piece at a time.

Why make it public?

For a long time, OtaconsKeep was simply my system.

It was built around my hardware, my network, my strange collection of ideas, and the way I wanted AI to work.

Eventually I realized that other people might want some of the same things.

Not necessarily my exact setup.

Not necessarily my agents.

But the ability to build something of their own.

A persistent AI environment. Local models. Memory. Voice. Automation. Agents with identities. A system that can grow instead of resetting every time you open a new conversation.

That is why the public version exists.

I want someone to be able to install OtaconsKeep, start small, and gradually build their own Keep.

Their hardware. Their models. Their agents. Their tools. Their world.

This is still an experiment

OtaconsKeep is a real working system, but it is also an ongoing experiment.

There are things that work extremely well.

There are things that are still being improved.

There are ideas that will turn out to be wrong.

There will be mathematical models that need tuning. Architectures that need redesigning. Tests that expose assumptions I did not realize I had made.

Models will improve. Hardware will improve. AI research will move. The system will change with it.

That is part of why the Engineering section of this site exists.

I do not want the interesting claims about OtaconsKeep to exist only as marketing language.

Where possible, I want to show the architecture, the requirements, the mathematical models, the risks, the test cases, the benchmarks, the failures, the mitigations, the behavioral experiments, and the evidence.

If an emotional model behaves a certain way, I want to understand why.

If a system fails, I want to know how.

If a feature is called reliable, there should eventually be evidence supporting that statement.

That engineering mindset is as much a part of OtaconsKeep as the AI itself.

Building the computer I wanted as a kid

When I look at the project now, the funny thing is that the original idea has not changed very much.

I still want my cyberdeck.

I still want to open the Codec and know someone is there.

I still want an AI that can help manage the systems around me.

I still want agents that remember what happened yesterday.

I still want technology that feels a little mysterious. A little futuristic. A little like stepping into the world that fascinated me as a kid.

The difference is that now I can actually build it.

The technology finally reached the point where one person with consumer hardware, open-source software, local models, enough curiosity, and an unreasonable amount of persistence can start turning those old science-fiction ideas into working systems.

OtaconsKeep is my attempt to do exactly that.

It is not finished.

I am not sure something like this is ever truly finished.

But every version gets a little closer to the machine I imagined all those years ago.
