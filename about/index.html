About Me

I started playing Metal Gear on the NES back in 1993.

I was a kid, and I fell in love with that world almost immediately. Around the same time, I was watching movies like Escape from New York, Escape from L.A., Blade Runner, The Matrix, and anything else that had that dark, noir, science-fiction view of the future.

Computers talking back to you. Artificial intelligence. Heads-up displays. Terminals. Networks. Machines that seemed to have personalities. Cyberdecks.

I always wanted my own.

At the time, of course, most of that was science fiction. But the idea never really went away.

From Engineering to AI

My background is engineering.

I've never really considered myself a traditional programmer or software engineer. I have worked with VBA, Python, C, and had some exposure to C++, but programming was always more of a tool for me than the destination.

I tend to approach software the same way I approach an engineering problem.

  • What are the requirements?
  • How do the pieces interact?
  • What happens when something fails?
  • How do you verify that it actually works?
  • How do you measure it?
  • How do you improve it?

Around 2018, I started getting much more serious about machine learning and artificial intelligence. I began working with tools like XGBoost and TensorFlow and started building systems that could take data, identify patterns, make predictions, and automate decisions.

One of my first real projects involved predictive-maintenance trend analysis.

That was when AI stopped being something I only saw in games and movies and started becoming something I could actually build.

Building the Thing I Always Wanted

Over the years I built plenty of experiments, automation projects, scripts, servers, and small AI systems.

But nothing came close to what eventually became OtaconsKeep.

OtaconsKeep has been roughly three years of building, breaking, rebuilding, experimenting, testing, and constantly asking one question:

What would the computer I always wanted actually look like if I could build it today?

Not just a chatbot.

Not just another web interface connected to an LLM.

I wanted a system.

I wanted different AI agents with their own roles and personalities. I wanted persistent memory. I wanted voice. I wanted automation. I wanted tools. I wanted the system to understand what was happening around it and be able to actually do something about it.

I wanted to be able to talk to my computer naturally and have something meaningful on the other end.

I wanted the Codec.

I wanted the cyberdeck.

So I started building it.

One piece at a time.

OtaconsKeep

Eventually all of those individual experiments started becoming one ecosystem.

Agents, local AI models, memory, voice, automation, routing, home-lab infrastructure, creative tools, computer control, and systems that could work together instead of existing as isolated applications.

That became OtaconsKeep.

The name and some of the inspiration obviously come from things I grew up loving, but the project itself has become something much bigger for me.

It is where my interests in engineering, artificial intelligence, systems design, science fiction, automation, and self-hosting all collided.

Because of my engineering background, I don't just want impressive demos. I want to know the system works — then prove it. The evidence lives in Engineering.

More Than a Chatbot

Most AI conversations still feel temporary. You talk, close the window, come back later, and the meaning is gone.

I wanted continuity. Memory should matter. Relationships should change. Experiences should leave a trace — something on the other end of the Codec that remembers you were there.

How that works (state, relationships, verification) lives in Engineering. This page is the why.

Why Make It Public?

For a long time, OtaconsKeep was simply my system.

It ran on my hardware, in my home lab, built around the things I wanted.

Eventually I realized that other people might want something like this too.

Not necessarily my exact Keep.

Their Keep.

Their own agents.

Their own hardware.

Their own personalities.

Their own automations.

Their own version of the system.

That is why I started turning OtaconsKeep into something other people could actually install and use instead of keeping it as a pile of software that only worked inside my house.

I'm also documenting the process because I don't want to pretend this appeared overnight or that every idea works perfectly the first time.

It doesn't.

There have been broken installers, bad ideas, failed experiments, architecture changes, things that worked in my lab and immediately broke on somebody else's computer, and plenty of moments where I had to go back and rethink the system.

That's part of building it.

And that journey is something I want to share.

Still Building the Cyberdeck

Technology finally reached a point where a normal person can build things that would have seemed ridiculous when I was a kid.

Consumer GPUs can run powerful AI models locally.

Open-source software can provide capabilities that once required massive organizations.

A home lab can contain an incredible amount of computing power.

Voice, vision, automation, agents, local models, and intelligent systems can all run inside your own home.

So more than thirty years after sitting in front of an NES playing Metal Gear, I'm still chasing basically the same idea.

I still want my cyberdeck.

The difference is that now I can actually build it.

OtaconsKeep is that journey.
